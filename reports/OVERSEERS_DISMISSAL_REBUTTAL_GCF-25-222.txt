I.  BOARD'S FACTUAL ERROR ON GABAPENTIN — REQUIRES CORRECTION AS A
    THRESHOLD MATTER

The dismissal (Page 3) states:

    "Contrary to your suggestion, Gabapentin is a Schedule Z drug in Maine
    and was so at the time of your arrest."

This statement is factually incorrect as a matter of law.

Gabapentin was NOT a scheduled controlled substance in Maine on August 2,
2018 — the date of the arrest. Gabapentin was added to Maine's Schedule Z
by:

    Maine Public Law 2019, Chapter 487
    "An Act To Schedule Gabapentin as a Schedule Z Controlled Substance"
    Effective date: October 15, 2019

The legislative history is publicly available from the Maine Legislature's
online database. The effective date is unambiguous: more than fourteen months
AFTER the conduct charged.

This is not a close legal question. A conviction for conduct that was not
criminal at the time of occurrence violates the Ex Post Facto Clause,
U.S. Const. art. I, § 10; Me. Const. art. I, § 11. See Collins v.
Youngblood, 497 U.S. 37 (1990); Rogers v. Tennessee, 532 U.S. 451 (2001).

Charging a defendant with gabapentin trafficking for conduct in August 2018
was legally impossible — and defense counsel's failure to raise this
meritorious, dispositive issue constitutes ineffective assistance of counsel
on its face. Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984).

The Board's dismissal on this point rests on a false premise. A charging
decision based on a legally impossible charge — a substance that was not
scheduled at the time — is not a reasonable exercise of prosecutorial
discretion under MRPC 3.8(a); it is a failure of basic legal competence.

The Board is requested to correct this error of law in any reconsideration.

II. THE BOARD IGNORED THE STATE'S OWN WRITTEN ADMISSION OF FABRICATED
    AGGRAVATION — THE CORE EVIDENCE IN THE FILE

The dismissal's analysis of Count 2 (Malicious Prosecution / Fabricating
Charges) makes no mention whatsoever of the November 6, 2025 letter from
current Assistant Attorney General Jason D. Anton, Esq. — the same attorney
copied on the dismissal letter itself.

That letter is in this Board's file. It constitutes the State of Maine's
own written admission that the Aggravated Trafficking charges were based on
an "inadvertent error" regarding the location of evidence — specifically, the
claim that drugs and firearms were found together in the vehicle.

To be precise about what the State admitted and what it means:

    STUVER'S CLAIM (July 15, 2025, Response to Board):
    "Three counts of Aggravated Trafficking...were based on the weight of
    the heroin and two firearms found in Mr. King's vehicle."
    (Also: "the presence of scheduled drugs within his vehicle...and the
    presence of the two firearms in his vehicle.")

    STATE'S OWN INDICTMENT (Ex. C, Counts 9-10, Place of Seizure):
    "33 Bald Mountain Drive, Bangor, Maine" — the RESIDENCE, not the vehicle.

    STATE'S ADMISSION (Anton letter, November 6, 2025):
    The claim that drugs were found with firearms in the vehicle was an
    "inadvertent error."

The legal significance is decisive. Maine's Aggravated Trafficking statute,
17-A M.R.S.A. § 1105-A(1)(C-1), requires that the firearm be possessed "in
furtherance of" the trafficking offense — a nexus that requires proximity.
Drugs in an apartment and firearms in a vehicle on Davis Road satisfy no such
nexus. The aggravation factor was legally unsupportable from the start.

These inflated Class A charges, later admitted to be false, were used by the
prosecution to threaten a four-year mandatory minimum sentence, coerce the
proffer, and pressure a guilty plea. The harm is direct and documented.

The Board's dismissal states: "there was no evidence presented to support
the allegation that the indictment was drafted maliciously." This finding
cannot stand when the Board's own file contains a letter from the State's
current prosecutor admitting the factual predicate for the most serious
charges was erroneous. That is not "no evidence" — that is a smoking gun.

MRPC 3.8(a) prohibits a prosecutor from "institut[ing] criminal charges that
the prosecutor knows or reasonably should know are not supported by probable
cause." Charging aggravated trafficking based on a firearms-drugs nexus that
does not exist — and that the State later admits was an error — raises a
direct question under MRPC 3.8(a) that the Board failed to analyze.

III. STUVER MADE A FALSE STATEMENT TO THIS BOARD — DIRECTLY WITHIN SCOPE

The dismissal (Page 5) states:

    "Attorney Stuver states that she is not aware of any search of
    Ms. Ross's cellphone that occurred."

This statement is directly contradicted by a document in this Board's file
and by public court records:

The Search Warrant issued August 7, 2018 (Docket No. SW-18-180, Maine
District Court, Southern Penobscot Division) — which Attorney Stuver's own
counsel cited as authority for the phone seizures — explicitly lists as a
target of the search:

    "Black LG Touch screen smartphone MEID (D) #A100003E98DD95. This phone
    is owned by Amanda Ross and currently held at Bangor Police Department."

The warrant proving the Ross phone was held and searched is a public court
record. It is the same warrant Stuver's counsel used to defend the legality
of the seizures. Stuver's counsel cannot simultaneously cite this warrant as
evidence of lawful procedure and allow her to claim she is "not aware of any
search of Ms. Ross's cellphone."

One of two things is true:

    (a) Attorney Stuver is not aware of the contents of the warrant she
        oversaw — raising serious competence concerns under MRPC 1.1; or

    (b) Attorney Stuver made a false statement to Bar Counsel — a direct
        violation of MRPC 8.4(c) (conduct involving dishonesty, fraud,
        deceit or misrepresentation).

Either way, this statement to the Board warranted scrutiny. Instead, Bar
Counsel accepted it without examination.

Furthermore, the Ross phone was seized on August 2, 2018 — before ANY
warrant authorized it. The residence warrant contained no authorization to
seize Amanda Ross's property. The subsequent August 7 warrant — obtained to
search a phone already illegally held — does not retroactively legalize the
initial seizure. Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963). Officer
Perry's seizure of the Ross phone on August 2 was warrantless. Stuver, as
supervising prosecutor, was responsible for that chain of custody.

IV. THE VIDEO TIMELINE IS IRRECONCILABLE WITH THE DISMISSAL'S FINDING

The dismissal (Page 2) states:

    "Attorney Stuver's counsel provided evidence demonstrating that Attorney
    Stuver made the video evidence available to your attorney within two weeks
    of your arrest."

The full dashcam video of the August 2, 2018 incident was not produced to
the Complainant until February 21, 2024 — five and a half years after the
arrest. This was confirmed through FOAA request. The footage shows:

    — No traffic infraction articulated at the stop
    — No warrant shown at the apartment door despite two direct requests
    — Officers producing a key from their own pocket to enter the apartment
    — A three-hour detention in 90-degree heat with a complained-of injury
      receiving no medical attention
    — Dashcam metadata (timestamp, GPS, officer ID) completely stripped
      from the produced file

If some video was produced "within two weeks," it was not the complete
footage. Selective early disclosure of partial video while withholding the
full recording for 5.5 years is itself a Brady violation. Brady v. Maryland,
373 U.S. 83 (1963). The withholding of material exculpatory video for nearly
six years is not addressed by the dismissal's finding.

The dismissal also states that Complainant's reply "asks the Respondent to
prove a negative" regarding unedited audio. To the contrary: where a
prosecutor controls the production of evidence, the burden to demonstrate
completeness rests with the State. The stripping of metadata from a dashcam
file is an affirmative act — not an absence of proof — and it is documented.

V.  THE BOARD FAILED ENTIRELY TO ADDRESS THE CONFLICT-OF-INTEREST ALLEGATION

The complaint raised the allegation, supported by documentation produced in
October 2025, that Attorney Stuver had previously represented the Department
of Health and Human Services / Child Protective Services (DHHS/CPS) in a
related family matter involving Keith King and his family while the criminal
case against him was simultaneously pending.

This allegation implicates MRPC 1.7 (Conflict of Interest: Current Clients)
and/or MRPC 1.9 (Duties to Former Clients), as well as the heightened duty
of a prosecutor under MRPC 3.8 to ensure fair proceedings. A prosecutor who
has represented an adverse state agency in proceedings against the same
individual's family — without disclosure — carries a potential institutional
bias that a defendant is entitled to know about and challenge.

The dismissal does not address this allegation anywhere in its twelve-point
analysis. This is a material omission. The omission itself warrants review.

VI. THE BOARD'S PERSONAL ATTACK ON THE COMPLAINANT'S SUBMISSIONS IS
    IMPROPER AND DOES NOT SUBSTITUTE FOR LEGAL ANALYSIS

The dismissal (Page 2, footnote 2) states:

    "I note that there are instances in your submissions to the Board where
    citations are not in alignment with your allegations, possibly due to
    the errors of a content generator you may have used."

And at footnote 2:

    "your December 16, 2025, email contains language indicating possible use
    of a content generator, as the following statement appears at the bottom
    of the e-mail: 'Would you like me to send this email now, or should I
    refine the section regarding the Davis Road stop further?'"

This observation — made twice — serves no legal purpose in the analysis. Bar
Counsel states she "reviewed your allegations under all applicable rules of
professional conduct, regardless of whether they were accurately cited."
If that is true, the source of the citations is irrelevant to the outcome.

Pro se complainants may use any lawful tool — word processor, legal research
database, or AI assistant — to prepare submissions. The use of writing
assistance does not affect the truth or falsity of the underlying facts, the
validity of the legal arguments, or the complainant's rights under the Maine
Bar Rules. Penalizing a disabled, unrepresented complainant for using
assistive technology while allowing the Respondent to be represented by a
licensed attorney (Jason Anton, Esq.) is a structural imbalance — not a
neutral observation.

The core factual contradictions in this complaint — drug location, firearms
location, gabapentin scheduling date, phone warrant timeline — do not require
a content generator to identify. They are established by the face of the
indictment, the police reports, and the State's own subsequent admissions.

     SERIOUS CONDUCT FROM REVIEW

Bar Counsel repeatedly states that the investigation is "limited to allegations
against Attorney Stuver." This is correct as to third parties. However, the
Board's scope limitations were used to avoid the most direct evidence of
Stuver's own misconduct:

    — Stuver's false statement to this Board (Sec. III above) is directly
      within scope — it is Stuver's own conduct before this very tribunal.

    — Stuver's filing of an indictment based on a firearm-drugs nexus she
      later admitted was erroneous is directly within scope under MRPC 3.8(a).

    — Stuver's pursuit of a gabapentin charge under a law that did not exist
      at the time of the conduct is directly within scope under MRPC 3.1
      (Meritorious Claims) and MRPC 3.8(a).

    — Stuver's undisclosed concurrent conflict of interest (DHHS/CPS) is
      directly within scope under MRPC 1.7 and 3.8.

None of these require analysis of third-party conduct. All are Stuver's own
actions. The Board's scope limitations should not have prevented their review.

VIII. THE BAIL ANALYSIS IGNORES ITS OWN LOGICAL IMPLICATION

The dismissal (Page 3) states that "the fact that the court subsequently
lowered your bail from $75,000 to $5,000 on your motion does not establish
that there was anything inappropriate with the initial bail amount."

This is circular reasoning. The court reduced bail by 93% — from $75,000 to
$5,000 — on a motion challenging its excessiveness. That reduction is judicial
validation of the excessiveness claim, not evidence against it. The original
bail was tied directly to the inflated Class A Aggravated Trafficking charges
that the State later admitted were based on an "inadvertent error." When the
factual predicate for the most serious charges was erroneous, the bail
premised on those charges was correspondingly unsupported.

IX. STUVER'S DEPARTURE IN DECEMBER 2019 DOES NOT INSULATE HER FROM
    RESPONSIBILITY FOR CONDUCT THAT OCCURRED WHILE SHE WAS THE PROSECUTOR

The dismissal repeatedly notes that Attorney Stuver left the AG's office in
December 2019 and was not present at the September 2021 plea. This is used
to deflect responsibility for the coercive plea.

The complaint does not allege that Stuver was present at the 2021 plea. It
alleges that Stuver's actions — specifically, the filing of false Aggravated
Class A charges based on a nonexistent firearm-drug nexus — created the
coercive pressure that her successor exploited to threaten a four-year
mandatory minimum and force the plea. Stuver built the weapon; Horn fired it.

An attorney who files charges they know or should know are unsupported by
probable cause cannot avoid professional responsibility for the downstream
consequences of those charges simply by leaving the office before the
resulting coercion takes effect.
