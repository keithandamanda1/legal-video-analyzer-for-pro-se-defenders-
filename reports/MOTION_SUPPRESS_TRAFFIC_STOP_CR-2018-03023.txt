                        INTRODUCTION

Defendant Keith A. King moves to suppress all evidence obtained as a result
of the August 2, 2018 traffic stop, residential search, and subsequent
seizures. The stop was pretextual and unrecorded; the search warrant was
signed hours after the stop had already begun; Defendant was transported
off-site without authority; officers entered his home with keys provided
by the housing authority — without a warrant, consent, or emergency — and
phone evidence was seized in direct violation of the warrant's exclusionary
clause.

All downstream evidence is constitutionally tainted fruit. Wong Sun v.
United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963).

                        FACTS

1. THE STOP (August 2, 2018 — approximately 12:56 PM)

    a. Officers stopped Defendant on Davis Road, Glenburn, Maine.
    b. No traffic infraction was articulated at the time of the stop.
    c. The stop was NEVER LOGGED in dispatch or radio — an off-book stop.
    d. The search warrant was signed approximately 2 HOURS after the stop
       began. The entire initial detention was warrantless.

2. OFF-SITE TRANSPORT (Davis Road → Defendant's Residence)

    Officers handcuffed Defendant at Davis Road and transported him to his
    home for execution of the residence warrant. Under Bailey v. United States,
    568 U.S. 186, 201-03 (2013), a residence search warrant does not authorize
    off-site seizure and transport of "recent occupants" unless the person is
    within the immediate vicinity when the search begins. Defendant was stopped
    miles from his apartment. The transport was unauthorized.

3. WARRANTLESS RESIDENTIAL ENTRY

    a. At the apartment door, Defendant asked for a warrant TWICE and was
       REFUSED BOTH TIMES (documented on dashcam video, released Feb. 2024).
    b. Officers produced a key from their pocket — provided by BHA (Bangor
       Housing Authority) without authorization — and entered without consent.
    c. The BHA master key log (produced October 2025, MHRC Exhibit B)
       confirms a key was issued to law enforcement on August 2, 2018 without
       a court order.
    d. This entry violated: (1) the Fourth Amendment; (2) 24 C.F.R. § 966.4(d)(3)
       (federal tenant rights in HUD-subsidized housing); (3) 5 M.R.S. § 4582
       (Maine right to privacy in housing).

4. PHONE SEIZURE IN VIOLATION OF WARRANT'S EXCLUSIONARY CLAUSE

    a. The search warrant contained an explicit clause EXCLUDING cellular phones.
    b. Officers seized Defendant's phone in direct violation of this clause.
    c. The phone was held August 2–7 without any warrant authority.
    d. A second warrant (August 7) sought to search the already-illegally-seized
       phone. That warrant is tainted fruit — it could not have been sought had
       the initial illegal seizure not occurred. Wong Sun, 371 U.S. at 484-85.

5. AMANDA ROSS PHONE — NO WARRANT EVER

    Officers seized Amanda Ross's phone for approximately 45 days without any
    warrant. The District Attorney falsely claimed the phone was never taken;
    forensic records prove a full data extraction was performed. This seizure
    was warrantless from inception.

6. DASHCAM TAMPERING / EVIDENCE SUPPRESSION

    The dashcam video was withheld for 5.5 years (released February 2024).
    When produced, the file was missing date/time, GPS coordinates, and officer
    identification — metadata stripped in violation of evidentiary obligations.
    California v. Trombetta, 467 U.S. 479 (1984); Arizona v. Youngblood,
    488 U.S. 51 (1988).

                        LEGAL ARGUMENT

I. THE INITIAL STOP LACKED REASONABLE ARTICULABLE SUSPICION.

Under Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), a stop requires specific, articulable
facts supporting reasonable suspicion. No traffic violation appears on camera.
The stop was not logged in dispatch or radio. The warrant was signed 2 hours
after the stop. The stop was pretextual and constitutionally deficient.

II. STOP EXTENSION VIOLATED RODRIGUEZ.

Even if the initial stop were valid, officers extended the stop beyond any
legitimate traffic mission without independent reasonable suspicion. Rodriguez
v. United States, 575 U.S. 348, 354-57 (2015) ("A police stop exceeding the
time needed to handle the matter for which the stop was made violates the
Constitution's shield against unreasonable seizures.").

III. OFF-SITE TRANSPORT WAS NOT AUTHORIZED BY THE RESIDENCE WARRANT.

Under Bailey v. United States, 568 U.S. 186 (2013), a residence search warrant
authorizes detention of persons found at the residence during the search, but
does NOT authorize off-site seizure and transport. Defendant was stopped miles
from his apartment, handcuffed, and transported there. This was an independent
constitutional violation.

IV. RESIDENTIAL ENTRY WITHOUT WARRANT OR CONSENT WAS UNCONSTITUTIONAL.

The Fourth Amendment protects the home above all else. Payton v. New York,
445 U.S. 573, 585 (1980). Officers entered Defendant's home after being denied
warrant confirmation twice, using keys obtained from BHA without legal authority.
There was no emergency, no consent, and no warrant at the time of entry.

V. PHONE EVIDENCE IS TAINTED FRUIT ON TWO INDEPENDENT GROUNDS.

    Ground A: The warrant itself excluded phones. Seizing a phone in direct
    violation of a limiting clause in a warrant is an unconstitutional search
    beyond the warrant's scope. Horton v. California, 496 U.S. 128 (1990).

    Ground B: The August 7 warrant depended on the illegal August 2 seizure.
    Without the illegal seizure, no second warrant could have been sought.
    Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963).

VI. SUPPRESSION OF ALL DOWNSTREAM EVIDENCE IS REQUIRED.

All evidence derived from the illegal stop, illegal transport, illegal entry,
and illegal seizures must be suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree:
    • Controlled substances allegedly found in the residence
    • Firearms allegedly found in the vehicle
    • All phone contents and forensic extractions
    • Any statements made during the 3-hour warrantless detention
    • The "admission" allegedly obtained by Officer Perry in an isolated,
      unrecorded, unwitnessed conversation
